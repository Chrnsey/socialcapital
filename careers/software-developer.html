Careers · Technology

Software developer

A well-paid job where, after your first role, people care far more about what you can build than where you studied.

Developers write the code behind apps and systems. It's more of a team job than people think. A lot of the day goes on reading other people's code, agreeing what to build, and fixing things that worked yesterday.

Ireland has far more of this work than you'd expect for its size. There are the big multinationals, and plenty of Irish companies you've probably never heard of.

Ways in

There is more than one. None of these is the "proper" one.

Computer science degree

The usual route

Three or four years through the CAO. It gives you the strongest grounding, and it's the easiest way onto the graduate programmes the bigger companies hire through.

How long
Three to four years
What it costs
Student contribution, often covered by SUSI

Apprenticeship

Less well known

Software and ICT apprenticeships exist in Ireland, but they don't get talked about much. You work and study at the same time, and finish with a qualification and real work experience.

How long
Two to four years
What it costs
You get paid

PLC, then work or a degree

Another way into a degree

A computing course in further education can lead straight into junior roles, or on to a degree through links with the universities.

How long
One to two years
What it costs
Usually low

Teaching yourself

Harder than it looks online

It does happen, and this industry cares more about what you can build than where you studied. But there's no structure and no one to ask, and you need finished projects to show. It works better as a second career than as a first job.

How long
Who knows
What it costs
Time

What you need

Points for computing courses vary a lot, whilst there is often a minimum Maths score required in the Leaving Cert. There has been a slowdown in graduate hiring in the computing field due to AI, which means internships and projects are highly important for getting a permanent contract after graduating.

What it pays

Starting salaries in tech are high compared with most first jobs in Ireland, and they rise quickly in the first few years. The apprenticeship route pays you from day one.

What people get wrong

“You need to have been coding since you were twelve.”

Plenty of developers started at eighteen, or thirty. Starting early helps, but it isn't a requirement.

“You need honours maths.”

Some courses want it, many don't, and most day-to-day work uses very little of it.

“It has to be a well-known university.”

After your first job, hardly anyone asks. Employers look at what you've built.
